\begin{abstract}
Causal and counterfactual reasoning have recently been given a category-theoretic semantics based on string diagrams and their graphical manipulation (``surgery''), yet no software supports this approach. We present a Julia prototype that performs counterfactual identification on probabilistic models represented as string diagrams, together with a scoped extension for causal inference computation. This lets us compare the categorical approach, conceptually and empirically, with existing R and Python implementations: an explicit diagrammatic intermediate representation achieves competitive performance while offering structural transparency and interpretability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Causal inference studies how interventions change the behaviour of complex systems \cite{pearl2009causality}, answering questions such as ``what is the effect of a drug on recovery?'' that statistical association alone cannot. Pearl formalises an intervention through the \emph{do}-operator: $P(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(X{=}x))$ is the distribution of $Y$ when $X$ is \emph{forced} to $x$ rather than merely observed to equal it. Computing such interventional effects is the basic task of causal inference, and it hinges on \emph{identification}: can the target effect be expressed in terms of the observable distribution at all? Under hidden confounding it may not; the ID algorithm and its counterfactual extension ID-CF \cite{shpitser2008complete} characterise when identification is possible.

Counterfactuals are more expressive still: they ask what would have happened under an alternate intervention, conditioned on observed evidence. A query $P(Y_{x}=y \mid X=x', Z=z)$ asks how the outcome would change if $X$ were set to $x$ when in fact $X=x'$ was observed. Grounded in Pearl's counterfactual semantics \cite{pearl2009causality}, such questions are central to explanation, responsibility attribution, policy evaluation, and fairness.

Recent work, initiated by~\cite{jacobs2019causal} and developed in \cite{jacobs2021causal,lorenz2023causal,tull2023activeinference,friend2025compositional}, gives causal inference a compositional semantics via category theory---notably \emph{Markov categories}~\cite{fritz2020synthetic,jacobs2015} and \emph{string diagrams}~\cite{intro_to_string_diagram}. Here a Bayesian network is not merely a graph but a morphism in a symmetric monoidal category, drawn as a string diagram whose wires are variables and boxes conditional mechanisms. Pearl's $\mathrm{do}$-operator is interpreted by \emph{string diagram surgery}~\cite{jacobs2019causal}: the box producing the intervened variable is cut out and replaced, making intervention a local graphical rewrite, not a global symbolic substitution. The same primitive drives both queries we target---interventional effects $P(Y\mid\mathrm{do}(X{=}x))$ (causal inference) and counterfactuals, where the model is duplicated into parallel worlds sharing their latent sources~\cite{jacobs2021causal}.

This semantics makes structural causal reasoning explicit: instead of manipulating symbolic formulas, one works with compositional diagrams whose topology encodes causal constraints, and surgery exposes the local structure of interventions and confounding. This makes it a natural candidate to explore as a basis for tool support, since the same diagrammatic primitives recur across model construction, intervention, and simplification.

Despite these theoretical developments, no single system performs causal reasoning---interventional-effect computation and counterfactual identification---directly on this semantics. Existing projects offer complementary functionality---categorical probabilistic libraries~\cite{cho2017efprob}, quantum-circuit tooling~\cite{kissinger2024pqs}, and general string diagram manipulators (e.g.~\cite{caldwell2026tensorrocq,chyp2024})---but a focused pipeline for diagrammatic causal inference and counterfactual identification is still missing. We take the first steps to address this gap. Our contributions are as follows.

\section{Categorical Background}\label{sec:background}

We recall just enough of the diagrammatic semantics of causal models to phrase the queries our tool answers, following the string diagram approach of~\cite{jacobs2021causal,jacobs2019causal}. We introduce each notion on one running example---a commuter's daily journey---and keep the category theory to the minimum needed to read the diagrams. We write capital letters such as $X,Y,Z$ for observable variables, and use the same symbols for their finite sets of possible values when they label wires in a string diagram. Lower-case letters such as $x,y,z$ denote particular values. The tensor product $\otimes$ is the monoidal product: in the finite probabilistic interpretation used here, it is the Cartesian product of value spaces. $I$ denotes the monoidal unit, i.e. no input.

\paragraph{Bayesian Networks as String Diagrams.}
Figure~\ref{fig:bn-string} renders our running \emph{commute} model as a string diagram (read bottom to top), its five observable variables labelling the wires: weather $W$, departure time $T$, mode of transport $M$, lateness $L$, and work performance $Y$. Each variable $X$ is produced by a \emph{mechanism} $f_X \colon \big(\textstyle\bigotimes_{V \in \mathrm{pa}(X)} V\big)\otimes \Omega_X \;\longrightarrow\; X$, drawn as a box: it reads $X$'s direct causes (its parents $\mathrm{pa}(X)$) together with a private noise source $\omega_X:I\to\Omega_X$---an exogenous state, drawn as a triangle, modelling everything left unmodelled---and outputs a distribution over $X$. Here $\Omega_X$ is the exogenous noise space for $X$, while $\omega_X$ is the state supplying a distribution over that space. In the commute example, the boxes $f_W,f_T,f_M,f_L,f_Y$ are the local causal mechanisms for weather, departure time, transport mode, lateness, and work performance, respectively. At a glance the diagram resembles a Bayesian network, but unlike a network it is an \emph{algebraic} object: read as a morphism of a symmetric monoidal category (SMC), it can be reasoned about compositionally. Boxes are morphisms, assembled by sequential composition and monoidal product (vertical and horizontal juxtaposition); variables are objects; states are morphisms with no input. Each object also carries a \emph{copy} map (black dot) duplicating a value and a \emph{discard} map deleting it; an SMC with these is a \emph{copy--discard} (or Markov) category~\cite{fritz2020synthetic,jacobs2015}, a basic framework for probabilistic computation. A copy dot is inserted wherever a variable feeds several mechanisms. \emph{Confounding}---a hidden common cause, written $T\leftrightarrow Y$---is a single latent state $R$ copied into both $f_T$ and $f_Y$: for the commuter, an unobserved factor (say, a recurring engineering work pattern) influences both when she sets off and how she performs.


%Composing all five mechanisms yields one diagram whose denotation is the joint distribution over $W,T,M,L,Y$. Routing every source of randomness through the explicit noise states $\omega_X$ leaves the mechanisms themselves \emph{deterministic}: a small move that pays off below, since two scenarios can then be made to share exactly the same randomness.


\paragraph{Causal Inference via String Diagram Surgery.}
Suppose we ask how the commuter's work performance $Y$ would change if her departure time were \emph{set} to $t$---the interventional effect $P(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(T{=}t))$. In Pearl's framework this is posed with the $\mathrm{do}$-operator and computed from the model and the observational distribution. Diagrammatically the intervention is \emph{string diagram surgery}~\cite{jacobs2021causal,jacobs2019causal,lorenz2023causal}: the mechanism $f_T$ is cut out and replaced by a state $\mathsf{do}(T{=}t):I\to T$ fixing $T$ to $t$ (the point distribution at $t$), recording that $T$ is now \emph{forced} rather than caused (Figure~\ref{fig:bn-surgery}). Severing $T$ from its causes while leaving the rest intact yields a diagram denoting $P(\,\cdot\mid\mathrm{do}(T{=}t))$; surgery is thus a \emph{local} rewrite, unlike the global symbolic substitutions of the algebraic account. The effect is then recovered from the diagram's existing conditionals by a \emph{comb disintegration}, whose details we omit~\cite{jacobs2021causal}; this procedure is the causal-inference component of our tool (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}).

\paragraph{Counterfactual Reasoning.}
Counterfactuals compare what happened with what \emph{would have} happened. For the commuter: \emph{given that she actually left at time $t'$ and would have taken mode $m$ under the prevailing weather, what would her performance $Y$ have been had she left at time $t$ instead?}---a query $P(Y_t \mid T{=}t',\,M{=}m)$. The factual and hypothetical are different worlds but concern the same commuter, hence share the same hidden causes. In the categorical approach~\cite{jacobs2021causal} this is represented via a \emph{twin} (or parallel-world) string diagram, obtained by placing the diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:bn-string}) for the factual world beside its $\mathrm{do}(T{=}t)$ surgery diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:bn-surgery}) for the counterfactual, but with the latent state $R$ appearing \emph{once} and feeding both rather than once per world. Sharing this single $R$ couples the worlds---the deterministic mechanisms then fix the same hidden circumstances across both---while the evidence $T{=}t'$, $M{=}m$ constrains the corresponding output wires. A query is \emph{identifiable} if its value is uniquely determined by the available distribution family: any two SCMs that agree on the available distributions must also agree on the query. Otherwise it is \emph{non-identifiable}. Whether the query is \emph{identifiable} is decided by the ID-CF algorithm~\cite{shpitser2008complete}: after surgery and simplification the diagram splits into maximal confounded blocks (the \emph{c-components}, or districts), whose diagrammatic counterparts are the \emph{R-fragments} the implementation extracts and rewrites next.

\paragraph{Why a Diagrammatic Perspective.}
The payoff of the categorical account is that everything above lives in \emph{one} language: the model, the counterfactual diagram, and the observable estimand it reduces to are all string diagrams in a Markov category, while an intervention $\mathrm{do}(X{=}x)$ is a local surgery operation transforming one diagram into another---rather than reasoning being split between a combinatorial graph for the model and a separate symbolic do-calculus. Because a diagram is a morphism, not merely a graph, the representation is \emph{compositional}: models are assembled from smaller pieces, and the operations on them---surgery, simplification, identification---are equational rewrites, sound by the laws of the category. One can therefore reason by structural induction and prove properties of a model --- a route to mechanised and, ultimately, formally verified reasoning.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}

We implemented our approach~\cite{idcf_tool} in Julia on the Catlab ecosystem~\cite{catlab}, which realises symmetric monoidal categories through \emph{wiring diagrams}, the concrete data structure for a string diagram. A model is supplied in its standard graphical form---an \emph{Acyclic Directed Mixed Graph} (ADMG), with directed edges for causal links and bidirected edges $X\leftrightarrow Y$ for hidden common causes (the confounding of Section~\ref{sec:background})---and the first stage compiles it into the string diagram of Section~\ref{sec:background}: each variable becomes a mechanism box, each bidirected edge a shared latent root. Equivalently the user may supply the model directly as a Catlab \texttt{WiringDiagram}, skipping this stage; ADMG compilation is just the front-end for graph-form input. Thereafter the main diagrammatic stages manipulate Catlab \texttt{WiringDiagram}s, in which boxes are mechanisms (morphisms) and wires variable dependencies. Each box carries a symbolic name encoding its role---\texttt{f\_X} a mechanism, \texttt{PU\_X} an exogenous source, \texttt{do\_X=x} an intervention, \texttt{obs\_X=x} an observation, \texttt{c\_X} a deterministic constant mechanism---so surgery and identification are uniformly rewrites on named boxes and their wiring.

\paragraph{Pipeline, Illustrated on a Worked Example.}
We follow our \emph{commute} scenario through the pipeline. The counterfactual query asks: for someone who left at time \(t'\) under weather \(w\), what would their work performance have been had they left at time \(t\), given that under the same weather they take mode \(m\)? A single entry point, \texttt{identify\_counterfactual}, runs five stages, timing each and exporting an intermediate diagram to Chyp after every one:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{ADMG compilation.} The input ADMG is turned into the string diagram of Section~\ref{sec:background}. It is first \emph{rootified}---each bidirected edge $X \leftrightarrow Y$ is replaced by a fresh latent root feeding both endpoints---and then each variable yields one mechanism box $f_V : (\mathrm{pa}(V), U_V) \to V$ and one exogenous source $PU_V$ (the noise state $\omega_V$ of Section~\ref{sec:background}). For the commute model the bidirected edge $T\leftrightarrow Y$ becomes the shared latent root $R$ feeding both $T$ and $Y$.
    \item \textbf{Parallel-world construction.} The base diagram is lifted into a multiverse by duplicating each mechanism once per world while \emph{sharing} the exogenous sources---the counterpart of shared exogenous noise. An intervened variable splits into a discarded sink and a fresh \texttt{do\_X=x} source; an observed variable gets an \texttt{obs\_X=x} box. The factual $Y$ and counterfactual $Y_t$ stay coupled through the shared root $R$ (the twin construction of Section~\ref{sec:background}).
    \item \textbf{Simplification.} Four rewrite passes reduce the diagram to canonical form: pruning discarded branches and dead boxes, separating observations from copy/fan-out, merging deterministic mechanisms with identical input sources, and absorbing exogenous sources into deterministic constant mechanisms where the absorption condition holds.
    \item \textbf{ID-CF identification} (Step~4). The simplified diagram is decomposed into \emph{R-fragments}---the diagrammatic counterpart of c-components (districts) in ADMGs---and each fragment is rewritten by case analysis into an interventional probability box $P(C_l, F_l^{out} \mid \mathrm{do}(D_l), \mathrm{do}(F_l^{in}))$. A residual unabsorbed exogenous source signals a non-identifiable query.
    \item \textbf{Expression output} (Step~5). The resulting probability boxes are parsed into a small expression tree and rendered as a LaTeX formula, with optional algebraic simplification (e.g.\ dropping vacuous interventions and eliminating normalised summations).
\end{enumerate}
Each stage is exported to Chyp; the simplified diagram and identified estimand for this example are in the \texttt{README.md} and under the configured trace directory, and full-resolution renders for every stage are collected in a companion gallery (\texttt{figure-gallery.pdf}) in the repository root. That README~\cite{idcf_tool}, the recommended starting point, maps the source module behind each stage, lists the data types, documents the supporting modules (Bayesian-network import, Chyp serialisation~\cite{chyp2024}, graph utilities), and gives a runnable quickstart for this pipeline and the comb disintegration examples below.

\paragraph{Comb Disintegration Causal Inference Extension.}
The pipeline above already \emph{identifies} interventional effects as the single-world special case of ID-CF; the prototype additionally includes a scoped extension that \emph{computes} them numerically, via comb disintegration~\cite{jacobs2021causal}, exposed through \texttt{infer\_causal\_effect}. Given a string diagram, a finite observational joint table, and a comb structure (context, intervention, bridge, and outcome variables; the full input contract and the smoking and HEPAR2 examples are in the README API), it checks that the diagram admits the comb decomposition and computes $P(C \mid A, \mathrm{do}(X))$ from the table. The diagram's role here is to \emph{certify} that comb disintegration applies and to fix its structure (which variables form the context, intervention, bridge, and outcome); the numbers are then assembled from the separately supplied joint table, while attaching a stochastic map to each box so that the joint is composed directly is left to future work. The pyAgrum comparison below thus measures the cost of computing the \emph{same} interventional distribution two ways---via the diagram-certified comb decomposition versus pyAgrum's variable-elimination \texttt{causalImpact}---not the cost of certification alone.

\paragraph{Design Decisions.}
The concrete data types---the input \texttt{ADMGModel}, the \texttt{ConfoundedModel} and \texttt{WiringDiagram} it compiles to, the per-world \texttt{CounterfactualQuery}, and the output term language---are documented in the README. From simplification onwards every state is a single \texttt{WiringDiagram}, so identification is pure pattern-matching on box names and rewiring, with no separate symbolic representation; and because the stages mirror the theoretical algorithm one-to-one, each is independently testable, the \texttt{test/} suite exercising every stage as its own module-level test set. Keeping each stage explicit and Chyp-exported makes every transformation inspectable, unlike symbolic tools where decomposition and simplification are internal. The README's visual companion (the figure gallery noted above) renders the commute example at every stage---input ADMG, compiled string diagram, parallel-world construction, simplified diagram, and identified estimand---so the reader can inspect the diagrammatic representation at each step.

\section{Empirical Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}
We evaluate along two dimensions: correctness, via a differential-testing harness against \texttt{cfid}~\cite{RJ-2023-053} (the reference R implementation of ID-CF on ADMGs), and runtime, against \texttt{cfid} and Python \texttt{y0}~\cite{hoyt2025y0}. The harness checks that our verdicts match an established tool; the benchmarks measure the cost of obtaining them.

\paragraph{Differential correctness testing.}
The harness generates counterfactual identification problems, translates each into the Julia and R \texttt{cfid} input formats, and compares the ID/FAIL verdicts. The corpus mixes structured benchmark cases with randomly sampled bow-free ADMGs over 5--8 nodes, each paired with a conditional counterfactual query; cases reducing to single-world interventional queries are filtered out. We use \texttt{cfid} 0.1.9 in the interventional-data setting, matching the implementation's scope.

Aggregated over six random seeds ($310$ cases each, $1860$ total; no tool errors, none dropped), Julia agrees with \texttt{cfid} on $1832$---a $98.5\%$ rate (per-seed $97.7$--$99.4\%$). The $28$ disagreements split $21$ where Julia returns FAIL and \texttt{cfid} ID, versus $7$ reversed. Neither direction is automatically a defect: a FAIL records a failure to find an identifying reduction, not a proof of non-identifiability, so disagreements either way are boundary cases---conservative limitations on either side---that we flag for investigation, not counterexamples to soundness.



\paragraph{Runtime benchmarking.}
Given this agreement, we compare runtime against \texttt{cfid} and \texttt{y0} (warm medians: 5 warm-up runs then 30 measured repetitions, excluding Julia's first-call JIT compilation).\footnote{Environment: Julia 1.11.6 (Catlab 0.17.2); R 4.5.2 (\texttt{cfid} 0.1.9); Python 3.14.2 (\texttt{y0} 0.2.11, pyAgrum 2.3.2); Intel i7-11800H, 16\,GB RAM, Windows 10.} For identifiable counterfactual queries, all three tools return symbolic estimands, not only Boolean verdicts. We use two inputs families: three standard queries---\texttt{drug}, \texttt{party}, and a conditional counterfactual query on the real-world \texttt{hepar2} network---and synthetic families of controllable size and topology (\texttt{chain}, \texttt{fanin}, \texttt{dense}, and a confounded chain \texttt{chain\_bi} with bidirected edges). The table below gives warm-median runtimes for the standard queries (top) and the synthetic families at $n=32$ (bottom); each speedup is the baseline runtime over Julia's, so $>1$ means Julia faster, $<1$ slower. \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

We present a prototype for counterfactual identification built on the categorical semantics of string diagram surgery, realising the full ID-CF pipeline as rewrites on Catlab wiring diagrams with a Chyp trace at every stage. The evaluation shows this is not merely feasible but competitive: it reproduces \texttt{cfid}'s results and is substantially faster on sparse and moderately dense models, at the cost of a simplification phase that bottlenecks on densely confounded graphs.
The implementation has four main limitations, to be addressed in future work:
\begingroup
\setlength{\leftmargini}{1.2em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
    \item \textbf{Scope:} the tool targets ID-CF for discrete graphical models under the usual assumptions---a correctly specified finite discrete ADMG with confounding as bidirected edges---and does not yet cover causal discovery or continuous latent variables; the bidirected-edge abstraction records confounding but not the full hidden-variable mechanism. Broadening the query class and integrating probabilistic inference engines is a natural next step.
    \item \textbf{Structure-dependent performance:} simplification relies on repeated full-graph traversals and dominates runtime, so on dense and especially bidirected-confounded graphs (e.g.\ \texttt{chain\_bi}) this preprocessing can erase or reverse the speedup; incremental rewriting and better wiring diagram data structures would remove these scans.
    \item \textbf{Visualisation:} Chyp export aids inspection but has a limited character set (symbols are mangled in labels, most acutely in identification outputs) and does not scale to large diagrams; a dedicated interactive visualiser (splicing, intervention, rewriting) would address this.
    \item \textbf{Verification and interoperability:} the explicit diagrams make every rewrite inspectable, but are not yet formally verified and lack native integrations. Future work: formal verification, integration with richer categorical and probabilistic toolchains (e.g.\ EfProb~\cite{cho2017efprob}), and links to causal-representation and reinforcement learning.
\end{itemize}
