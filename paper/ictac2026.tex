\begin{abstract}
Causal inference and counterfactual reasoning have recently been given a category-theoretic semantics based on string diagrams and their graphical manipulation (``surgery''). Despite these theoretical advances, there is currently no software support for this approach. In this work, we present a prototype implementation in Julia that enables causal and counterfactual queries to be evaluated on probabilistic models represented as string diagrams. This allows us to compare the categorical approach, both conceptually and empirically, with existing implementations in R and Python. In particular, we show that the use of an explicit diagrammatic intermediate representation can achieve competitive performance while offering greater structural transparency and interpretability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Causal inference is the study of how actions and interventions change the behaviour of complex systems \cite{pearl2009causality}. Unlike purely statistical association, causal inference seeks answers to questions such as ``what is the effect of a drug on recovery?'' or ``would a different decision have produced a different outcome?''. Central to this enterprise is the problem of \emph{identification}: given a causal model and available data, can a target interventional or counterfactual query be uniquely expressed in terms of observable quantities? In the presence of hidden confounding, this question becomes non-trivial; the classic ID algorithm and its extension to counterfactual queries, ID-CF \cite{shpitser2008complete}, provide the theoretical foundations for when identification is possible.

Counterfactuals are an especially expressive class of causal queries. They ask what would have happened under alternate interventions, often conditioned on observed evidence. A typical counterfactual query takes the form $P(Y_{x}=y \mid X=x', Z=z)$, asking how the outcome would change if $X$ were set to $x$ when in fact $X$ was observed to be $x'$. Such questions are essential in explanation, responsibility attribution, policy evaluation, and fairness, and they are grounded in the counterfactual semantics introduced by Pearl \cite{pearl2009causality}. They also lie at the heart of many applications in medicine, social science, and automated decision-making, where the ability to compare actual and hypothetical worlds is crucial.

Recent work, initiated by~\cite{jacobs2019causal} and further developed in \cite{jacobs2021causal,lorenz2023causal,tull2023activeinference,friend2025compositional}, has shown that causal inference can be given a rigorous compositional semantics using category theory, most notably the theory of \emph{Markov categories}~\cite{fritz2020synthetic,jacobs2015} and of \emph{string diagrams}~\cite{intro_to_string_diagram}. In this perspective, a Bayesian network is not merely a graph, but a morphism in a symmetric monoidal category, represented graphically as a string diagram where wires indicate variables and boxes indicate conditional mechanisms. Interventions are then modelled as local rewrites of these diagrams (called `surgeries' in~\cite{jacobs2019causal} because they replace parts of the diagram), and counterfactual constructions (first introduced in~\cite{jacobs2021causal}) correspond to pairs of string diagrams that share latent sources.

The advantage of this semantics is that it makes structural causal reasoning explicit. Rather than manipulating symbolic formulas alone, one works with compositional diagrams whose topology encodes causal constraints. Diagram surgery exposes the local structure of interventions and confounding, and it admits reasoning principles that are naturally compositional. This makes the semantics attractive for both theoretical study and tool support, because the same diagrammatic primitives can be re-used across model construction, intervention, and simplification.

Despite the growing theoretical prominence of string diagram surgery, there is not yet a single system that implements counterfactual identification directly on this semantics. Existing projects provide complementary functionality — categorical probabilistic libraries~\cite{cho2017efprob}, quantum-circuit tooling~\cite{kissinger2024pqs}, and general string-diagram manipulators (e.g.~\cite{caldwell2026tensorrocq,chyp2024}) — but a focused pipeline for diagrammatic counterfactual identification is still missing. In this work we take the first steps to address this gap. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We present an implementation of counterfactual identification based on string diagram surgery, realised in Julia with the Catlab ecosystem.
    \item We analyse the concrete challenges of implementing categorical causal semantics, including diagram representation, parallel world construction, and simplification.
    \item We connect this implementation to the broader body of work on string diagrammatic reasoning, with explicit support for Chyp export, and we describe how the same diagrammatic infrastructure can be extended toward richer probabilistic semantics in future work.
    \item We compare our implementation with existing R and Python alternatives, identifying the cases where a categorical approach performs better or worse and clarifying the trade-offs involved.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews the categorical background and string diagram view of Bayesian networks. Section~\ref{sec:implementation} describes the implementation choices and available functionality. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} compares the tool with existing work and highlights experimental evidence. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Categorical Background}\label{sec:background}

We work in the setting of \emph{Markov categories}~\cite{fritz2020synthetic} (also called cd-categories), the categorical framework underlying the string-diagram approach to causality of Jacobs, Kissinger and Zanasi~\cite{jacobs2019causal,jacobs2021causal}. This section fixes the diagrammatic vocabulary that the implementation in Section~\ref{sec:implementation} manipulates directly.

\subsection{Causal Models as String Diagrams}
A Markov category is a symmetric monoidal category in which every object $A$ carries a \emph{copy} map $A \to A\otimes A$ and a \emph{discard} map $A \to I$ to the monoidal unit, subject to the usual comonoid axioms; together with a \emph{uniform} source $I\to A$ these constitute the copy--discard--uniform (CDU) structure~\cite{fritz2020synthetic,jacobs2015}. Morphisms are drawn as \emph{string diagrams}~\cite{intro_to_string_diagram}: wires denote variables, boxes denote morphisms, sequential composition stacks boxes and the tensor places them side by side. A morphism $f:A\to B$ interpreted as a conditional distribution $P(B\mid A)$ is a \emph{channel}; a \emph{state} is a channel $I\to A$ (a distribution on $A$) and an \emph{effect} a morphism $A\to I$, of which the discard map is the canonical one. A morphism is \emph{deterministic} when it commutes with copying; the two cases we use are \emph{sharp states} (deterministic states, fixing a variable to a constant) and \emph{sharp effects} (constraining a wire to an observed value).

In this language a structural causal model is assembled compositionally. Each endogenous variable $X$ with parents $\mathrm{pa}(X)$ becomes a \emph{mechanism}
\[ f_X : \Big(\textstyle\bigotimes_{Y \in \mathrm{pa}(X)} Y\Big)\otimes U_X \;\longrightarrow\; X, \]
a box that takes the parent wires together with a fresh exogenous noise source $U_X$ (a state $I\to U_X$) and produces the wire $X$. Wiring each mechanism's output into the inputs of its children---inserting copy maps wherever a variable feeds several children---yields a single string diagram whose denotation is the joint distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:string_diagrams}). Two structural advantages follow: conditional-independence (d-separation) properties surface as diagrammatic connectivity, and, crucially for us, interventions become \emph{local} rewrites rather than global symbolic substitutions.

Latent confounding is treated through \emph{Acyclic Directed Mixed Graphs} (ADMGs), whose bidirected edges $X\leftrightarrow Y$ record a shared hidden cause. Mirroring the implementation's compilation step, each bidirected edge is \emph{rootified}: it is replaced by an explicit latent root state feeding both $X$ and $Y$ through a copy map, turning implicit confounding into a shared exogenous source.

\subsection{Surgery and Counterfactuals}
An intervention $\mathrm{do}(X=x)$ is modelled by \emph{string diagram surgery}~\cite{jacobs2019causal,jacobs2021causal,lorenz2023causal}: the wires entering $f_X$ are \emph{cut}---the incoming parents are discarded---and $X$ is instead supplied by a sharp state $\mathsf{do}(X{=}x):I\to X$ fixing it to the constant $x$ (Figure~\ref{fig:original_model}). Categorically this replaces a channel by a constant morphism; graphically it severs $X$ from its causes while leaving the rest of the diagram intact, so the resulting diagram denotes the interventional distribution $P(\,\cdot \mid \mathrm{do}(X{=}x))$.

Counterfactual queries such as $P(Y_x \mid X{=}x',\,Z{=}z)$ compare what \emph{did} happen with what \emph{would have} happened under a different intervention, and so require several worlds to coexist. They are captured by a \emph{parallel-world} (multiverse) construction: the model is duplicated once per world, surgery is applied only in the intervened world(s), and---the key point---the exogenous noise sources are \emph{shared} across worlds rather than re-sampled. Diagrammatically the latent state is copied into every world (Figure~\ref{fig:parallel_world}), so the worlds remain coupled through their common randomness; this is exactly the parallel-world semantics of counterfactual identification~\cite{pearl2009causality,shpitser2008complete}. Observed evidence ($X=x'$, $Z=z$) is imposed by attaching sharp effects to the corresponding output wires.

Whether a counterfactual query is \emph{identifiable}---expressible purely in terms of the observational distribution---is decided by the ID-CF algorithm~\cite{shpitser2008complete}. After surgery and simplification, the diagram decomposes into maximal confounded components, the \emph{c-components} (or districts) of the ADMG; their diagrammatic counterparts are the \emph{R-fragments} that the implementation extracts and rewrites. This decomposition makes plain why some queries are identifiable and others are not: identification succeeds exactly when every fragment's exogenous noise can be absorbed into a consistent, observable expression.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}

We implemented the approach in Julia on top of the Catlab ecosystem~\cite{catlab}, which provides a computational realisation of symmetric monoidal categories through \emph{wiring diagrams}. The whole computation manipulates a single kind of object: a Catlab \texttt{WiringDiagram} in which boxes are mechanisms (morphisms) and wires are variable dependencies. Each box carries a symbolic name encoding its role---\texttt{f\_X} for a mechanism, \texttt{PU\_X} for an exogenous source, \texttt{do\_X=x} for an intervention (a \emph{sharp state}), \texttt{obs\_X=x} for an observation (a \emph{sharp effect}), \texttt{c\_X} for a deterministic constant mechanism---so that surgery and identification are realised uniformly as rewrites on named boxes and their wiring.

\subsection{Pipeline and Artifact}
Identification is driven by a single entry point, \texttt{identify\_counterfactual} (alias \texttt{run\_cf\_pipeline}) in \texttt{src/id\_cf.jl}, which executes five stages, timing each independently and exporting an intermediate diagram after every stage (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{ADMG compilation} (\texttt{src/admg\_compile.jl}). An input Acyclic Directed Mixed Graph (ADMG) is \emph{rootified}---each bidirected edge $X \leftrightarrow Y$ is replaced by a fresh latent root feeding both endpoints---and then compiled by \texttt{graph\_b\_to\_scm} into a base structural diagram with one mechanism box $f_V : (\mathrm{pa}(V), U_V) \to V$ and one exogenous source $PU_V$ per variable.
    \item \textbf{Parallel-world construction} (\texttt{build\_multiverse}). The base diagram is lifted into a multiverse diagram by duplicating each mechanism box once per world while \emph{sharing} the exogenous source boxes across all worlds---the diagrammatic counterpart of shared exogenous noise. An intervened variable splits into a discarded sink and a fresh \texttt{do\_X=x} source; an observed variable receives an \texttt{obs\_X=x} sharp-effect box.
    \item \textbf{Simplification} (\texttt{src/simplify\_cf.jl}). Four rewrite passes reduce the diagram to canonical form: pruning discarded branches and dead boxes, separating sharp effects from copy/fan-out, merging deterministic mechanisms with identical input sources, and absorbing exogenous noise by replacing each $f_X$ with a deterministic constant mechanism $c_X$.
    \item \textbf{ID-CF identification} (Step~4, \texttt{src/id\_cf.jl}). The simplified diagram is decomposed into \emph{R-fragments}---the diagrammatic counterpart of c-components (districts) in ADMGs---and each fragment is rewritten by case analysis into an interventional probability box $P(C_l, F_l^{out} \mid \mathrm{do}(D_l), \mathrm{do}(F_l^{in}))$. A residual unabsorbed exogenous source signals a non-identifiable query.
    \item \textbf{Expression output} (Step~5, \texttt{id\_cf\_step5}). The resulting probability boxes are parsed into a small expression tree and rendered as a LaTeX formula, with optional algebraic simplification (e.g.\ dropping vacuous interventions and eliminating normalised summations).
\end{enumerate}
The remaining modules support this pipeline: \texttt{src/bn\_import.jl} and \texttt{tools/import\_bn\_to\_admg.jl} ingest external Bayesian-network files (\texttt{.bif}/\texttt{.net}) as ADMGs; \texttt{src/chyp\_export.jl} serialises any diagram to the Chyp format~\cite{chyp2024} for inspection; and \texttt{src/utils.jl} provides the shared box predicates and graph utilities (topological ordering, signature matching). The command-line driver \texttt{src/run\_demo.jl} and the worked commute example in \texttt{src/main.jl} exercise the full pipeline.

\subsection{Data Structures and Design Decisions}
Causal models are described by an \texttt{ADMGModel} (directed and bidirected edge lists), which rootification turns into a \texttt{ConfoundedModel} (directed edges plus a map from each latent root to the variables it confounds). A \texttt{CounterfactualQuery} specifies, per world, the interventions, observations, and output variables. From the simplification stage onwards every state is a Catlab \texttt{WiringDiagram}, and identification proceeds entirely by pattern-matching on box names and rewiring---there is no separate symbolic graph representation. Final expressions are built over a small term language (atomic probability factors, products, sums, and fractions) so that identifiable queries are reported as algebraic formulas matching the output style of existing identification tools, and non-identifiable queries report the stage and box at which absorption failed.

Three decisions characterise the design. First, a \textbf{single diagrammatic representation} is used end to end, so surgery, simplification, and identification are all rewrites on the same object rather than transformations between distinct data models. Second, the pipeline keeps \textbf{every intermediate stage explicit} and exports it to Chyp, making each transformation step inspectable---in contrast to symbolic tools where decomposition and simplification are internal. Third, the stages mirror the theoretical algorithm one-to-one, so each can be tested and optimised independently; the test suite in \texttt{test/} covers ADMG compilation, multiverse construction, simplification, R-fragment partitioning, and expression generation as separate module-level test sets.

\section{Empirical Evaluation}\label{sec:discussion}

We evaluate the prototype against \texttt{cfid}~\cite{cfid_package}, the reference R implementation of ID-CF on ADMGs, and against the Python \texttt{y0} library. The benchmark harnesses live in \texttt{comparisons/runtime/} (\texttt{julia\_benchmark.jl}, \texttt{r\_cfid\_benchmark.R}, \texttt{python\_y0\_benchmark.py}); all timings are warm medians over repeated executions on a standard desktop, with the Julia and R measurements aligned so that the Julia identification cost (Step~4 $+$ Step~5) is compared against R's \texttt{identifiable($\cdot$)} call. We use two families of inputs: three standard queries---\texttt{drug}, \texttt{party}, and a conditional counterfactual query on the real-world \texttt{hepar2} network---and synthetic graph families of controllable size and topology (\texttt{chain}, \texttt{fanin}, \texttt{dense}, and a confounded chain \texttt{chain\_bi} carrying bidirected edges).

On the standard benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:speedup}, top) the categorical tool is consistently faster than \texttt{cfid}: by roughly $2.7\times$ on the small \texttt{drug} and \texttt{party} queries and by $86\times$ on the larger \texttt{hepar2} network. A stage-level breakdown explains why. The dominant cost in our pipeline is the explicit \emph{simplification} stage (for \texttt{drug}, $16.7$ of $19.85$\,ms), while identification itself (Steps~4--5) is comparatively negligible; \texttt{cfid}, conversely, spends almost all of its time inside \texttt{identifiable}. The categorical method thus trades a heavier but reusable diagram-simplification phase for a very light identification phase, and this trade pays off increasingly as the model grows: on chains the speedup rises monotonically from $1.14\times$ at $n=8$ to $5.93\times$ at $n=32$.

The synthetic families also expose where the trade-off does \emph{not} pay off (Table~\ref{tab:speedup}, bottom). On sparse and moderately dense graphs the categorical tool wins by $3.5$--$6\times$, but on the heavily-confounded \texttt{chain\_bi} family the result reverses: at $n=32$ our tool is only $0.65\times$ as fast (i.e.\ slower), because simplification cost is driven up by bidirected confounding rather than by the identification work that \texttt{cfid} performs. On this family the Python \texttt{y0} library is in fact faster than both. We report this honestly: the categorical approach is competitive-to-faster on sparse and moderately dense models, but its simplification stage currently penalises it on densely confounded graphs.

For correctness we use cross-tool validation. Across all identifiable benchmark queries our tool and \texttt{cfid} return expressions denoting the same interventional/counterfactual distribution (up to ordering and formatting), and both correctly report failure on the non-identifiable queries. Beyond runtime, making string diagrams first-class objects gives the tool a form of \textbf{semantic transparency} absent from symbolic implementations: every transformation step---ADMG compilation, multiverse construction, each simplification pass, and fragment identification---is exported to Chyp and can be inspected, whereas in \texttt{cfid} decomposition and simplification are internal. The use of Catlab and Chyp also connects the implementation to the categorical and diagrammatic-reasoning communities rather than positioning it purely within the causal-inference software ecosystem.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a prototype for counterfactual identification on Bayesian networks built directly on the categorical semantics of string-diagram surgery. The artifact realises the full ID-CF pipeline---ADMG compilation, parallel-world construction, diagrammatic simplification, R-fragment identification, and expression output---as rewrites on Catlab wiring diagrams, with a Chyp trace exported at every stage. The evaluation shows this is not merely feasible but competitive: the tool reproduces \texttt{cfid}'s results and is substantially faster on sparse and moderately dense models, at the cost of an explicit simplification phase that becomes the bottleneck on densely confounded graphs.

The current implementation has limitations that guide future development:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scope:} the tool targets the ID-CF setting for discrete graphical models and does not yet cover causal discovery or continuous latent-variable models. Its validity also rests on the usual causal-model assumptions (causal sufficiency, correct specification of dependencies), and the bidirected-edge abstraction may not capture every hidden dependency.
    \item \textbf{Structure-dependent performance:} the simplification stage relies on repeated full-graph traversals and dominates runtime; on dense and especially bidirected-confounded graphs (e.g.\ \texttt{chain\_bi}) this preprocessing cost can erase, or reverse, the identification speedup.
    \item \textbf{Visualisation:} Chyp export aids inspection but supports a limited character set (unsupported symbols are mangled in labels, which is most acute for identification outputs) and does not scale visually to large diagrams.
    \item \textbf{Verification:} the explicit intermediate diagrams make every rewrite step inspectable, but the steps are not yet formally verified, and there are no native integrations with other categorical/probabilistic toolchains in this release.
\end{itemize}

Future work falls into four directions. First, \textbf{optimising simplification} through incremental rewriting and more efficient wiring-diagram data structures, to avoid the repeated full-graph scans that penalise dense and confounded models. Second, \textbf{broadening the query class} and integrating with probabilistic inference engines. Third, building a dedicated \textbf{interactive string-diagram visualiser} (supporting splicing, intervention, and rewrite operations) to replace Chyp's limitations. Fourth, exploring \textbf{formal integrations} with richer categorical and probabilistic toolchains---for example EfProb~\cite{cho2017efprob}---and connections to causal representation learning and reinforcement-learning pipelines. These directions would help the prototype evolve from a focused research artifact into a broadly interoperable toolchain for categorical causal reasoning.
